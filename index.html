What is an event camera?

A normal camera takes a full picture many times a second, whether or not anything changed. An event camera (also called a dynamic vision sensor, or DVS) has no frames. Each pixel works on its own and reports only when the brightness it sees changes. Every report is an event: the pixel's position, a timestamp, and a polarity, ON if the pixel got brighter or OFF if it got darker.

A still scene produces almost no data. A moving edge produces a thin line of events that follows it. Hardware sensors timestamp events to the microsecond, react to very fast motion without blur, and handle very bright and very dark areas in the same scene.

How this page emulates one

Each webcam frame is shrunk to a small grid (320 × 240 by default) and turned into brightness values. For each pixel the page works with the log of brightness, L = ln(Y + ε), because real event pixels respond to relative change (contrast), not absolute change.

Every pixel remembers a reference level. When L − ref ≥ C the pixel fires an ON event and the reference moves up by C. A big change fires several events, one per step of C. Darkening works the same way and gives OFF events. C is the contrast threshold slider: lower it and the sensor gets more sensitive and noisier.

Two more pieces copy real sensors. A refractory period stops a pixel from firing again too soon. A background-activity filter drops an event if none of the pixel's 8 neighbours fired recently. Real motion lights up neighbouring pixels together, while sensor noise shows up as isolated dots.

The frequency readout splits the image into 16 × 16 pixel cells. For each cell it keeps the last 3 seconds of ON minus OFF counts, adds them up to rebuild that cell's brightness over time, and runs an FFT four times a second. Cells with a clear peak are coloured by frequency.

What a webcam can't do

This is an emulation built from frames, so it inherits the frame rate. A 30 fps webcam takes a sample every 33 ms, and events inside one frame interval get spread across it, not truly measured. By the sampling theorem, nothing faster than half the frame rate (the Nyquist limit, 15 Hz at 30 fps) can be measured. Faster flicker doesn't vanish. It folds back and shows up as a false, lower frequency. Tick "Add a 22 Hz square" in the synthetic demo to see a 22 Hz blink read as 8 Hz.

A webcam also applies auto-exposure, compression and motion blur, none of which a real event pixel has. Event counts and noise here won't match any particular sensor.

Why it matters: telling drones from birds

A drone's propellers spin at thousands of RPM, so the blades flicker at hundreds of hertz or more. A bird's wings beat at a few hertz to a few tens of hertz. A real event camera, with microsecond timestamps, can measure that blade frequency directly, even when the drone is only a few pixels across. That rotor signature is a strong cue for spotting drones and telling them apart from birds.

A webcam can't see propeller frequencies, since they sit far above its Nyquist limit. What this page shows is the same method at a slower scale: per-region event counts, a frequency transform, and a map of where the periodic motion is. Wave your hand, tap a finger, or point the camera at a blinking LED.
